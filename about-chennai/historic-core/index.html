LOCALITY HISTORY & LAND DEVELOPMENT

What published sources tell us

This guide summarises the history supported by the cited sources. Neighbourhood-wide history is distinguished from parcel-level details, which can only be confirmed through individual records.

1639–1640 · Settlement and Fort

The founding nucleus of English Madras

The Greater Chennai Corporation’s published history says Francis Day explored the coast in 1637 and that the grant for Madraspatnam is dated August 1639. It records the English party’s arrival on 20 February 1640 and the start of the Fort St. George factory building on 1 March 1640. The same account says the fort was completed in 1653. These dates describe the Company settlement and fort—not the beginning of all human settlement in the Chennai region.

Before and around the Fort

Madraspatnam, Chennapatnam and the growing town

The Corporation history distinguishes the pre-existing Madraspatnam village north of the Fort from the settlement growing around the Fort, which was also called Chennapatnam in early accounts. It describes the two built-up settlements gradually joining. The source itself notes that the exact origins of the name “Madras” are disputed; this page therefore does not present one etymology as settled fact.

Trade, crafts and town formation

Commercial growth was not just a colonial story

The same history describes Indian merchants, artisans, weavers and other workers settling around the early town, including a reported concentration of weaver families in Madraspatnam. This helps explain the relationship between the Fort, the northern town and the wider textile and trading economy. Any more precise street-by-street map should be tied to a dated plan or archival record.

George Town and Parry’s Corner

George Town and Parry’s Corner: how the commercial core developed

The historic commercial core developed across connected places: Fort St. George, the older town north of the Fort, George Town, Parry’s Corner, Broadway and streets serving the port. Markets, warehouses and commercial streets changed at different times. The available sources support the broad town-development story, but a precise date for each street or subdivision should only be stated when a period map, gazetteer, municipal record or building history confirms it.

Research findings: how the historic core actually grew

Madras did not begin as a fully formed city, and the modern metropolitan area was not created by one developer. Fort St George became the English East India Company’s fortified commercial base after the 1639 grant; construction of the Fort began in 1640. Around it, the Company’s settlement, Indian merchant communities, artisans, port-facing trade and older Tamil coastal settlements developed through different processes. The official Greater Chennai Corporation history is the starting chronology, while the 1939 History of the City of Madras and the 1961 Census city report provide contemporary historical descriptions and maps.

George Town, Black Town and Parry’s Corner

The settlement north of the Fort was historically called Black Town and later George Town. It was a dense commercial and residential district shaped by merchant streets, community institutions, warehouses, markets and access to the harbour. Under Governor Thomas Pitt (1698–1709), the old Black Town was fortified and surveyed; the GCC account describes the town walls, gates, canal and the distinction between Muthialpet and Peddunaickenpet. Under Governor Macartney (1781–1785), Black Town assumed a shape closer to the later street pattern. These are documented town-formation milestones—not evidence that a single modern real-estate promoter laid out all of George Town.

Chintadripet: a documented planned craft settlement

The GCC historical account records that in October 1734 the government resolved to establish a weaving town on the garden associated with Sunkurama, with settlement conditions for spinners, weavers, washers, painters and associated households. A grant was issued and Bemala Audiappa Narayana helped populate the settlement; the account says it had nearly 250 families within two years. This is a stronger, specific example of an organised settlement than a general claim that all early Chennai neighbourhoods were laid out in the same way.

Mylapore, Triplicane, Santhome and the southern suburbs

The 1939 city history describes Triplicane, Santhome and Mylapore as suburbs connected by older roads, and notes that the Mount Road and Poonamallee Road had military origins. It also describes garden houses with wide verandahs and large compounds spreading beyond the crowded Fort area from the mid-18th century. This helps explain the transition from a compact fort-and-port town to a city of institutions, compounds, villages and radial roads. It does not establish the exact date or promoter for every later street or subdivision.

What the records establish

Finding: The historic core has a traceable town-development narrative, but parcel-level promoter names, exact subdivision dates and historical sale prices require individual deeds, approved plans, survey maps or registration records. No locality-wide historical price has been inferred from the age of a neighbourhood.
